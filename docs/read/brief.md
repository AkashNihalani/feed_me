# Read as a strategist's brief (Oct 23 2026)

The question: Read has the intelligence (fingerprints, blind-tested rules, twins, follows against likes) but no
cohesion and no human tone. It should feel like a strategist. It has to work for whoever follows the account (the
owner, a rival, a brand, someone learning) and for any kind of account (Nike, Netflix, Apple, MrBeast, a beauty brand,
a comedian). What should we do?

## The short answer

1. **Make the read an argument, not a set of cuts.** Every read opens with one thesis and five beats in a fixed order,
   each following from the last: *what carries it → what it hides → what it costs → what moved → what to watch*, then
   one take for whoever is reading. That is the stories' content. Everything under it is evidence for one of the beats.
2. **Give every part of the page one question.** The brief (what's the read?), the Feeder File (what is it made of,
   and what pays?), Runs (how did we get here?), Ranks (which posts?), the post mortem (what happened in this one?).
3. **One writer for the front door.** A brief pass after each run, reading the run's state plus code's strategic
   numbers (effort against return, concentration, likes against follows, twins inside a lane). It replaces the three
   passes that write front-door text today (now, questions, drops). Prompt, pack and check are written and tested on
   three real accounts (below).
4. **Ask the relationship once, use it once.** "Why are you watching @x? It's me / a rival / someone I might work with /
   learning." The argument is the same for everyone; only the last story (the take) changes.
5. **Adapt through the obvious driver.** For every account the read first names what carries it (the beginner's beat:
   a face, a series, a title, a result, a YouTube video) and then must find something *inside* it (the veteran's beat).
   That one move is what makes the same six beats fit Nike, Netflix, Apple and MrBeast.
6. **Stack the briefs into a feed read.** What a beauty brand watching its industry pays for is the read across the
   accounts it follows: what works for several of them, what costs all of them, what only one has found. The briefs
   share a vocabulary of plays, so code can line accounts up and one more pass writes it. Tested on the three reads we
   have (below).

## Why it doesn't cohere today

**It was built from the data outward.** Each layer of the pipeline got its own surface: rules, things, runs, ranks,
findings, questions, stories. Each is a different cut of the same evidence, and each is written as a full explanation.
Nothing on the page makes an argument, so the reader has to assemble one.

**The front door is buckets, not a point.** Today's Anuj stories, read in a row:

> One reel carries Anuj, and it was an argument. · Naming a fight and saying it himself lifts him. · Paid posts that
> open on the product sink. · His second-biggest reel grew him nothing. · His next news take keeps the government
> reel's crowd. · (what his posts are made of) · A deal holds when the joke comes before the product · His posts do
> best when he's the one talking · Playing both sides of a fight viewers already have wins

Every line is true; none follows from the one before. The best thing in the read (the 154K-like gag grew nothing while
the political take grew him by 1,796 in a day) is split across two buckets ("Feeding" and "Odd"), so it never lands as
one point. Then three rule stories repeat what the buckets said.

Lakmē has the same shape: "Three Aneet sketches carry the whole account" (story 2) and "Showing the viewer's bad habit
first, then the fix, wins" (story 7) are one insight (it isn't Aneet; episode two had her too and did 640) told six
stories apart, so neither the beginner nor the veteran gets the "oh".

**The sentences were compressed into a machine's voice.** A 9-word cap on claims gave "lifts him", "sink", "wins".
A person says "What grows him is picking a side on the news."

**Four takes on every claim.** Each story carries a "so" for the team, a rival, a partner and a learner: about 40 short
advice lines per read, most of them generic. A strategist writes the diagnosis once and the advice once, for the person
in front of them.

**It starts from patterns, not from the account's job.** A strategist's first question is what the account needs from
Instagram (sell, launch, get booked, grow, fill a store, promote a show) and whether what it posts does that. Read
starts from what lands; it never says what the account is trying to do, so "what's working" has nothing to be working
*for*.

**Three writers for the front door.** The now pass, the questions pass and the drops each decide separately what
matters most, which is how the same point shows up in a slot, a question and a rule.

## What a beauty brand watching its industry actually needs

Whoever follows an account wants the same diagnosis; they act on it differently.

| Who's reading | What they're asking | What they do with the same brief |
|---|---|---|
| **The account itself** | What's working, what's wasting posts, is our bet paying, what next | Double down, cut, brief the next post, show the boss proof |
| **A rival** | What's their edge, can we copy or counter it, where are they exposed, what are they about to do | Take the format, avoid their strong lane, hit their weak one |
| **A brand or agency choosing partners** | What does a deal with them get, whose crowd is it, which formats hold a brand | Book (or don't), brief the right format, price it |
| **Someone learning the category** | What works here right now and why | Steal the move behind the hit |

So the argument is written once, about the account, in the third person, and only the take changes. The same fact
cuts both ways: "one actress, four episodes in" is a risk to the owner and an opening to the rival.

And the person in the example (a beauty brand tracking competitors and creators) isn't really asking about one account.
They want: who's getting attention in my category right now, what's working across several of them, what's dying,
what only one of them has figured out, which creators actually move a brand, and where I stand. That is the feed read
(section below). The per-account brief is its raw material.

## The brief

Six stories in the same order every time, then the take and the map. Each story is a post full bleed, one number and
one sentence; the "why" opens under it (the story player you have).

| # | Story | What it says | The rule it follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The read** | A thesis with a tension in it (≤12 words) + who the account is and what it needs from Instagram | A claim someone could disagree with, never a summary |
| 2 | **What carries it** | The obvious driver, with the number that shows its weight | What a top-posts tool would also tell you: the beginner's beat |
| 3 | **What it hides** | What a veteran would miss, found inside or under the driver | Same driver, different result (a twin); or the numbers disagree (likes against follows); or effort upside down. Never "the person" when the driver is a person |
| 4 | **What it costs** | Where it spends posts, deals or money and gets little back | The number is effort against return ("9 of 30 posts, 2% of the likes") |
| 5 | **What moved** | The biggest change since the last read; settles the last call in the first person | "I said …; it held / it didn't, because …" |
| 6 | **What to watch** | One test the next posts will pass or fail, with the number to beat | Hedged, never a forecast |
| 7 | **Your take** | One decision for the person reading (owner / rival / partner / learning) | A decision, not advice-shaped filler |
| 8 | **The map** | The door to the Feeder File | |

**The paragraph test.** Read the thesis and the five claims in a row. They must sound like one person making one point.
If a claim can be cut without the next one losing its sense, it isn't part of the argument. This is the cohesion test,
and a judge can run it.

**Rules stop being stories.** The rule that explains story 3 is linked from it; all rules live on the map and their own
pages, with their records. That alone removes the repetition between the slots and the rule stories.

## The page: one question per part

| Part | Its question | What it owns | What it doesn't do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The brief** (stories) | What's the read? | The argument, the take | List patterns; retell rules |
| **The Feeder File** (layer) | What is it made of, and what pays? | Things, the rules (mechanisms) with their records, effort against return per thing | Make the argument |
| **Runs** (wall) | How did we get here? | The diary: each run's take, what changed in the read, calls settled | Re-explain rules |
| **Ranks** (wall) | Which posts? | The scoreboard | Prose |
| **Post mortem** | What happened in this post? | The beats, where it landed, what it beat | Explain the account |

Every story links down into one of these ("On the map", "Post mortem", "Run 3"). Nothing below links back up with
words of its own; the brief is the only place that argues.

## Any account: the obvious driver, then inside it

The six beats never change. What changes by account is what usually carries it, where "inside it" is, what usually
costs, and what the read can't see. Code finds the driver (the split that explains most of the likes); the reader has
to look inside it. That is the beginner/veteran split in one move: the beginner learns what drives the account, the
veteran learns what drives *that*.

| Account | What it needs from Instagram | What usually carries it (story 2) | Where "inside it" is (story 3) | What usually costs (story 4) | What the read can't see | A run |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Comedian / creator** (Anuj) | Grow, get booked | A few hits; one persona or format | Likes against follows; same format, different result | Deals that play as ads; others' bits | Sends, profile visits | 10 posts |
| **Beauty brand** (Lakmē) | Sell, launch | A face or a series | Same face, different result: what the big ones do that the flat one didn't | Catalogue posts, the day-after recap, the brand toasting itself | Paid reach | 10 posts or 14 days |
| **Retail brand** (Kendra Scott) | Fill the store, sell the drop | Its plainest product shots | Inside one format: an occasion against a launch | Retelling one event; launch montages | Paid reach, store sales | 10 posts or 14 days |
| **Nike** (brand with athletes) | Heat, then sell the drop | The athlete, and whether they just won | For the same athlete: the real moment against the made ad; Nike's own posts without a face | Product drops with no person | The result, the athlete's news, the athlete's crowd on co-posts | 14 days |
| **Netflix** (a slate of titles) | Make people press play | The title's fandom | Inside one title: cast candid against trailer against meme edit | Trailers for small titles | How the title is doing off Instagram | 7 days (10 posts is two days) |
| **Apple** (rare, polished posts) | Make the product wanted | Launch weeks | Outside launches: which Shot on iPhone photos travel (who took it, what kind of scene) | Feature explainers | Paid reach | 14 days or 10 posts |
| **MrBeast** (a YouTube engine) | Send people to the video, sell Feastables | How the video did on YouTube | Which cuts travel on Instagram alone: a premise you get in one frame against one that needs the video | Product posts that aren't a bit | YouTube, said once as a limit | 10 posts |
| **A sports team** (the Lakers) | Heat around games, sell tickets and merch | The result and the star | Within wins (and within losses), which post travels; partner posts as deals | Sponsor slots | The game itself | Per game week |

How each of these would open, as the questions the read answers (no data for them yet, so no claims):

- **Nike.** Carries: is it the athlete or the moment? Inside: for the same athlete, does the real game emotion beat
  the produced film, and by how much? Costs: what do drops with no athlete get? Can't see: the result and the athlete's
  own crowd on co-posts, said once.
- **Netflix.** Carries: which titles took the week? Inside: within the biggest title, which kind of post travelled
  (the cast being themselves, the meme cut, the trailer)? Costs: what did the small titles' trailers get? Can't see:
  viewing.
- **Apple.** Carries: launch weeks, said plainly. Inside: between launches, which photographs travel and what they
  share (the photographer, the scene, the light)? Costs: which posts explain features to nobody?
- **MrBeast.** Carries: the video on YouTube, said as a limit. Inside: which cuts travel on Instagram without the
  video? Costs: Feastables posts that aren't a bit. Watch: whether the next cut that needs the video sinks.

What has to be built for these (from the Oct 22 review, still open): page types and post jobs in code, the driver per
type (title, athlete, result as fields the fingerprint reads), co-posts held apart as borrowed crowds, the follower
signal test (at Nike's size a day's follows don't move with a post, so the read says so once instead of guessing), run
length by time as well as posts.

## How it should sound

A strategist who watched the feed, talking to a smart friend: conclusions first, the evidence right behind them.

| Instead of (today's stories) | Say (generated, round 3) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One reel carries Anuj, and it was an argument. | Keeps posting ads; gets followed for mocking the government. | A thesis with a tension (effort against return), not a fact |
| Three Aneet sketches carry the whole account. | The reel that shows the habit in two seconds got 48× episode two's likes. (Same series, same star.) | The veteran's beat sits inside the beginner's |
| Recap carousels waste the day after a hit. | Swatches and boards fill 9 of 30 posts and return 3% of the likes. | Effort against return, said as a cost |
| Name real people in your angriest post. (one of four takes on each of nine stories) | Open on a bad habit viewers recognise by second three, then fix it; skip the title card and recap slides. (one take, for the person reading) | Advice once, for someone |

Rules for the voice (in the prompt): tension over summary; the account's own yardstick ("65× its usual", "1 of 9 beat
the usual"), never percentiles; people, not metrics (send, save, scroll past, follow, wince); plain verbs; one point of
view ("I said … and it held") when it earns its place; name the trade-off and the risk; describe every post cited;
whole sentences with articles and verbs. Never: engagement, resonate, content, pillar, leverage, insight, compelling,
authentic, storytelling, viral, "the key is", exclamation marks, questions, emojis, "top X%".

## The pipeline, with one writer per job

| Step | Who | What it writes | Status |

| **Brief pack** | Code | Effort against return by lane, concentration, deals, the follower line summarised, likes against follows, twins inside a lane, and the numbers beyond likes (below) | New: `read_brief_pack.py` + `read_brief_metrics.py` |
| **Brief** | Sonnet, after each run | The argument: thesis, five beats, four takes, plays | New: `read_brief_prompt_v4.md` |
| **Brief check** | Code (+ a Haiku judge later) | Numbers the pack printed, ids, one post per beat, lengths, banned words, plays from the library; prints the paragraph test | New: `read_brief_check.py` |
| **Feed pack** | Code | Every play across accounts, against each account's own usual | New: `read_feed_pack.py` |

The brief replaces the now pass, the questions pass and the drops (three writers of front-door text become one). A
brief is about 8k tokens in and 1.5k out: roughly ₹1.5 on Sonnet 5.5 batch, less than the passes it replaces. The feed
brief is about the same once a week.

## Tested: three rounds on three accounts (Oct 22–23)

Sonnet, one agent per account, the pack after run 3 (30 posts each), the prompt, nothing else. Outputs in
`apps/worker/scripts/out/read_brief/` (`_v1/`, `_v2/` kept; the live `brief.json` is the latest round).

- **Round 1 (prompt v1).** All three read as one argument; the paragraph test passed on every account. Two flaws:
  the prompt's examples were Anuj's own lines, and his thesis came back as an echo of them ("Booked for deals,
  followed for…"); a rival's take came back as a timing trick ("post on his deal days"). Rule: prompt examples come
  from made-up accounts only, and a rival's take is about the rival's own posts.
- **Round 2 (prompt v2).** Fixed both, capped the number's label at 8 words (it sits on the picture), and told the
  reader to prefer likes-against-follows over a craft point. That overcorrected: all three built "what it hides" on
  the follower line. True for Anuj (+1,796 on the day of one post, against a usual +16), meaningless for Lakmē and
  Kendra Scott, where at 2.8M and 1.6M followers no day can be pinned on a post. Lakmē lost its best insight (same
  Aneet, same series: 640 against 30,749 likes) to "the biggest reel came on a usual day for followers".
- **Round 3 (prompt v3).** Code now says in the pack whether a follower day can be pinned on a post ("Signal: yes" only
  when a standout day had a post on it), and followers are evidence only then. No thumb on the scale for which kind
  of insight wins. The cover must be the post the claim is about.

Lesson for the pipeline: code decides what counts as evidence (here, the follower signal test from the Oct 22 review);
the prompt only says how to argue. Every time the prompt tried to rank kinds of insight, the reads bent toward that
kind.

## v9: the math layer, post mortems and runs that teach (Oct 23)

The user: too much rides on likes and comments; wants evidence and math ("1 in 9 posts"), posting time, beating the last
5/10/30/50, the feeder's evolution; post mortems ("why it landed here", "what it beat") teach nothing; runs need
breaking down. Simulated again from zero on Sonnet 5.5 (medium effort), three runs per account, applied to the app
(backups `apps/web/src/data/*.v8.json`).

- **The ledger** (`read_ledger.py --upto N`): per post, its standing against the last 5/10/30/50 posts of its format and
  how rare its result is; per account, form 10 posts at a time, the last 5/10/30/50 against each other, 1 in N go
  2×/5×/10×, each format's rate, and time of day and weekday with a Bonferroni-adjusted binomial test (printed as
  standing out only when chance is under 1 in 10; the rest as "could be chance"). The old unguarded timing, spacing and
  length patterns are gone from the packs.
- **Run reader v7** (`read_run_prompt_v7.md`, harness `read_v9_run.py`): uses the ledger; every post names its sibling,
  the one difference (with timestamps) and its lesson; every run names one or two contrasts. Bug found and fixed: the
  link-mender turned the new id fields into links; `sibling`, `a` and `b` are now exempt and all runs were re-applied.
- **Brief v6** (`read_brief_prompt_v6.md`): leans on the ledger; "what changed" carries the account's direction.
